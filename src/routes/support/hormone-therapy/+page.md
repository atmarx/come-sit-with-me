<Cite id="nams-position-2022" tooltip="The Menopause Society. The 2022 Hormone Therapy Position Statement of The North American Menopause Society. Menopause. 2022;29(7):767-794.">Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment</Cite>
for <a href="/symptoms/hot-flashes">hot flashes</a> and <a href="/symptoms/sleep-wont-come">night sweats</a>, and for the vaginal and urinary symptoms of menopause. And for twenty years, many women who could have benefited from it were denied access—or scared away—because of a study that was badly misinterpreted.

Let me tell you what actually happened, and what we now know.

## The WHI Controversy

<Cite id="whi-initial-2002" tooltip="Writing Group for the WHI Investigators. Risks and Benefits of Estrogen Plus Progestin in Healthy Postmenopausal Women. JAMA. 2002;288(3):321-333.">In 2002, the estrogen-plus-progestin arm of the Women's Health Initiative was halted early</Cite>, and the news terrified women and doctors alike. Headlines screamed about increased breast cancer, heart attacks, strokes.

Hormone therapy use fell sharply. An entire generation of women suffered through symptoms they didn't need to suffer through.

Here's what got lost in the panic:

**The study population was wrong for the question.** <Cite id="whi-followup-2013" tooltip="Manson JE, et al. Menopausal Hormone Therapy and Health Outcomes During the Intervention and Extended Poststopping Phases of the WHI Randomized Trials. JAMA. 2013;310(13):1353-1368.">The average age of participants was 63. Many were more than a decade past menopause.</Cite> This is completely different from treating a 48-year-old in perimenopause.

**The hormones used were specific formulations.** The study used only oral conjugated equine estrogen (Premarin) and medroxyprogesterone acetate (Provera). We now know these aren't optimal—but the results were applied to all hormone therapy.

**The absolute risks were small.** <Cite id="whi-followup-2013" tooltip="Manson JE, et al. Menopausal Hormone Therapy and Health Outcomes During the Intervention and Extended Poststopping Phases of the WHI Randomized Trials. JAMA. 2013;310(13):1353-1368.">Among 10,000 women taking the combined pills for a year, there were about 6 more coronary events, 9 more strokes, 9 more blood clots in the lung and 9 more breast cancers, against 6 fewer colorectal cancers and 6 fewer hip fractures. The WHI authors still concluded the risks outweighed the benefits for this regimen, but those are the absolute numbers behind the headlines.</Cite>

**Benefits were ignored.** <Cite id="whi-initial-2002" tooltip="Writing Group for the WHI Investigators. Risks and Benefits of Estrogen Plus Progestin in Healthy Postmenopausal Women. JAMA. 2002;288(3):321-333.">The study actually showed decreased fractures, decreased colorectal cancer</Cite>, and—<Cite id="whi-estrogen-alone-2004" tooltip="WHI Steering Committee. Effects of Conjugated Equine Estrogen in Postmenopausal Women with Hysterectomy. JAMA. 2004;291(14):1701-1712.">in the estrogen-only arm—no increased breast cancer risk</Cite>.

## The Reassessment (2024-2025)

Twenty years of subsequent research, including major analyses from Nature, Yale, the Korean Society of Menopause, and the International Menopause Society, have dramatically shifted the picture:

**The timing hypothesis has support, not proof.** <Cite id="whi-reanalysis-2007" tooltip="Rossouw JE, et al. Postmenopausal Hormone Therapy and Risk of Cardiovascular Disease by Age and Years Since Menopause. JAMA. 2007;297(13):1465-1477.">In a secondary look at the WHI trials, women who started hormone therapy closer to menopause tended toward lower heart disease risk than women who started later, but that trend fell short of the study's own bar for significance, and stroke risk was raised regardless of timing.</Cite> <Cite id="elite-2016" tooltip="Hodis HN, et al. Vascular Effects of Early versus Late Postmenopausal Treatment with Estradiol. New England Journal of Medicine. 2016;374(13):1221-1231.">In ELITE, oral estradiol started within 6 years of menopause slowed the thickening of the carotid artery wall compared with placebo; started 10 or more years after menopause, it didn't. It had no significant effect on coronary CT measures in either group, so this is a signal about artery walls, not proof of fewer heart attacks.</Cite> <Cite id="keeps-trial-2014" tooltip="Harman SM, et al. Arterial Imaging Outcomes and Cardiovascular Risk Factors in Recently Menopausal Women: A Randomized Trial. Annals of Internal Medicine. 2014;161(4):249-260.">I'll be honest that the trials don't all sing the same note: in KEEPS, four years of hormone therapy started near menopause neither slowed nor sped the thickening of artery walls—no clear protection, but no harm either.</Cite>

**Transdermal estrogen may be safer than oral.** Patches, gels, and sprays bypass the liver's first pass, and observational studies suggest a lower risk of blood clots than oral estrogen—though head-to-head trials are lacking. They also don't raise blood pressure like oral estrogen can.

**Micronized progesterone is preferred.** Unlike synthetic progestins, micronized progesterone (Prometrium) has a better safety profile, helps with [sleep](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come), and may have a lower impact on breast cancer risk.

**The FDA has changed its stance.** <Cite id="fda-ht-boxed-warning-2025" tooltip="U.S. Food and Drug Administration. HHS Advances Women's Health, Removes Misleading FDA Warnings on Hormone Replacement Therapy. November 2025.">In late 2025 the FDA announced it would remove the boxed warnings on heart disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia that scared women and providers for two decades</Cite>—<Cite id="fda-ht-relabel-2026" tooltip="U.S. Food and Drug Administration. FDA Approves Labeling Changes to Menopausal Hormone Therapy Products. February 12, 2026.">and in February 2026 the first revised labels were approved, including for vaginal estrogen.</Cite>

## Current Evidence

| Outcome | Finding | Timing Matters? |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | Started closer to menopause: a trend toward lower heart disease risk, short of statistical significance; started later: a trend toward higher risk. Stroke risk raised either way | Possibly—not proven |
| **Breast cancer** | Small absolute risk increase with combined estrogen + progestin; estrogen-only may not increase risk | Duration matters |
| **Bone health** | Prevents fractures; effect is maintained while taking HT | Any age |
| **Cognition** | Largest analyses find no effect on dementia risk—neither protection nor harm | Not a reason to start or avoid |
| **[Depression](/symptoms/mood)** | Can help perimenopausal depression—average benefit is modest | During transition |
| **[Hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes)** | Most effective treatment available | Any stage |
| **[Vaginal symptoms](/symptoms/dryness)** | Highly effective; local estrogen is safe for most | Any stage |

## Types of Hormone Therapy

### Estrogen Delivery

**Transdermal (patches, gels, sprays):** Generally preferred. <Cite id="nams-position-2022" tooltip="The Menopause Society. The 2022 Hormone Therapy Position Statement of The North American Menopause Society. Menopause. 2022;29(7):767-794.">Observational studies suggest a lower risk of blood clots than oral estrogen, and it bypasses the liver's first pass—though head-to-head trials are lacking.</Cite> More stable blood levels than oral. Options include patches (Climara, Vivelle-Dot), gels (EstroGel, Divigel), and sprays (Evamist).

**Oral pills:** Observational studies suggest a higher risk of blood clots than transdermal, though head-to-head trials are lacking. Affects liver proteins (including clotting factors). May work better for some women; metabolism varies.

**Vaginal (local):** For <a href="/symptoms/dryness">vaginal and urinary symptoms</a> specifically. <Cite id="vaginal-estrogen-safety" tooltip="ACOG Clinical Consensus No. 2. Treatment of Urogenital Symptoms in Individuals With a History of Estrogen-dependent Breast Cancer. Obstetrics & Gynecology. 2021;138(6):950-960.">A low-dose option for many women who can't take systemic hormones. For breast cancer survivors, it's a conversation to have with your oncologist: nonhormonal options come first, and vaginal estrogen is generally reserved for when those haven't helped. If you take an aromatase inhibitor, that decision belongs to you, your gynecologist, and your oncologist together. Data so far don't show an increased risk of recurrence, but experts still describe real uncertainty.</Cite> Comes as creams, tablets, rings, or suppositories.

**And a new estrogen is on the horizon.** <Cite id="estetrol-eu-approval-2026" tooltip="Gedeon Richter. Richter Receives European Commission Approval for FYLREVY (Estetrol Tablet) as Hormonal Replacement Therapy for Oestrogen Deficiency Symptoms in Postmenopausal Women. March 27, 2026.">In March 2026, Europe approved a hormone therapy built on estetrol, a natural estrogen made by the fetal liver during pregnancy.</Cite> <Cite id="estetrol-e4comfort-2026" tooltip="Simoncini T, et al. Estetrol (E4) for the Treatment of Moderate to Severe Vasomotor Symptoms in Postmenopausal Women (E4COMFORT I). Maturitas. 2026;209:108965.">In a phase 3 trial it clearly reduced hot flashes compared to placebo.</Cite> It isn't approved for menopause in the US, and it was studied in postmenopausal women rather than during perimenopause—but after decades of the same few options, it's good to see genuinely new ones arriving.

### Progestogen (required if you have a uterus)

If you have a uterus, you need progestogen to protect the uterine lining from estrogen stimulation. Without it, estrogen alone increases risk of endometrial cancer.

**Micronized progesterone (Prometrium):** Body-identical progesterone. Better side effect profile than synthetic progestins. May help [sleep](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come) (take at bedtime). Probably lower breast cancer risk than synthetic progestins.

**Synthetic progestins (MPA, norethindrone):** More side effects—mood changes, bloating—for some women. The type used in the WHI study.

**Hormonal IUD (Mirena):** Provides local progestogen protection to the uterus. Can be combined with estrogen therapy. Good option if you also need contraception (yes, you may still need [contraception during perimenopause](/considerations/contraception)).

### Testosterone

Testosterone isn't FDA-approved for women in the US, but it's used off-label and prescribed routinely in other countries for [low libido](/symptoms/libido), fatigue, and wellbeing. If this interests you, discuss it with your provider—preferably one knowledgeable about menopause.

## Who Can and Can't Use Hormone Therapy

**Generally good candidates:**
- Women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause
- Significant symptoms affecting quality of life
- No contraindications (see below)

**Absolute contraindications:**
- History of breast cancer (though local vaginal estrogen may still be an option—a conversation to have with your oncologist)
- History of blood clots (though transdermal may be considered)
- Active liver disease
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding
- Known or suspected pregnancy

**Situations requiring careful consideration:**
- Family history of breast cancer
- History of stroke or heart disease
- Migraine with aura
- Gallbladder disease
- High triglycerides

If you have a complex medical history, find a provider who specializes in menopause—not one who reflexively says no because they're not comfortable with the nuances.

## Starting Hormone Therapy

Some practical considerations:

**It takes time to work.** Full effect for hot flashes may take 4-8 weeks. Don't give up too quickly.

**Dosing may need adjustment.** Many women start on a standard dose and adjust based on response. Some need more; some need less.

**There may be side effects initially.** Breast tenderness, bloating, spotting—these often settle within a few months. <Cite id="nice-guidelines-2015" tooltip="National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Menopause: Identification and Management. NICE Guideline NG23. 2015, updated April 2026.">Unscheduled bleeding after the first 6 months, or more than 3 months after a dose or preparation change, deserves a prompt check—UK guidance now says so plainly—so tell your provider rather than waiting it out.</Cite>

**You don't have to stay on it forever.** The decision about duration is individual. Some women use it for a few years during the worst of the transition; others stay on it longer. There's no mandatory stop date.

**You can taper or stop outright.** <Cite id="nice-guidelines-2015" tooltip="National Institute for Health and Care Excellence. Menopause: Identification and Management. NICE Guideline NG23. 2015, updated 2026.">Tapering may limit symptoms coming back in the short term; over the longer term, it makes no difference to symptoms which way you stop.</Cite>

## Finding a Knowledgeable Provider

Many providers are still operating on outdated WHI-era fears. You deserve someone who:

- Understands the timing hypothesis
- Knows the difference between formulations
- Takes your symptoms seriously
- Discusses both risks AND benefits
- Treats you as a partner in decision-making

See [Finding Care](/finding-care) for how to find and advocate with providers.
