[Hormone therapy](/support/hormone-therapy) isn't right for everyone. Some women can't take it for medical reasons. Some don't want to. Some have tried it and found it didn't work for them. And some simply want to know what else exists.

The good news: there are real alternatives. Some are genuinely new—breakthroughs that didn't exist a few years ago. Others have been around longer but are worth knowing about.

## The Breakthrough: NK3 Receptor Antagonists

This is exciting. For the first time, we have medications that treat <a href="/symptoms/hot-flashes">hot flashes</a> by directly targeting the <a href="/whats-happening/brain">brain pathway</a> tied to them—<Cite id="kndy-thermoregulation-2013" tooltip="Rance NE, et al. Modulation of Body Temperature and LH Secretion by Hypothalamic KNDy Neurons: A Novel Hypothesis on the Mechanism of Hot Flushes. Frontiers in Neuroendocrinology. 2013;34(3):211-227.">the KNDy neurons in the hypothalamus, which research has implicated in hot flashes</Cite>.

These drugs don't provide estrogen. They don't affect your hormone levels at all. They simply act on that pathway instead.

### Fezolinetant (Veozah)

**FDA approved May 2023**

- First NK3 receptor antagonist approved for hot flashes
- <Cite id="fezolinetant-skylight" tooltip="Johnson KA, et al. Efficacy and Safety of Fezolinetant in Moderate to Severe Vasomotor Symptoms Associated With Menopause: A Phase 3 RCT. Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism. 2023.">About 64% fewer moderate-to-severe hot flashes by week 12 on the 45 mg dose (placebo brought about 45%), in a trial of women already past menopause</Cite>
- Works directly on that same pathway
- 45mg once daily
- **Important:** Requires liver enzyme monitoring (check before starting and periodically). <Cite id="fezolinetant-realworld-2026" tooltip="Hsu CD, et al. Utilization of Fezolinetant for the Treatment of Moderate-to-Severe Vasomotor Symptoms of Menopause in a Real-World Setting. Menopause. 2026;33(5):529-535.">In the first large real-world study, fewer than half of the women staying on the drug actually got those liver tests—please don't be one of them</Cite>

For women who can't or won't take hormones but have significant hot flashes, this was the first genuinely new option in decades. <Cite id="fezolinetant-nma-ht-comparison-2024" tooltip="Morga A, et al. Network meta-analysis: fezolinetant vs. HT and non-HT for VMS. Menopause. 2024;31(1):68-76.">A 2024 network meta-analysis in postmenopausal women, sponsored by the drug's maker, found fezolinetant's reduction of hot flash frequency was not significantly different from any US-available hormone therapy—and significantly better than all other non-hormone options studied.</Cite>

### Elinzanetant (Lynkuet)

<Cite id="elinzanetant-approval-2025" tooltip="Bayer press release. U.S. approval of a new hormone-free treatment option for moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms due to menopause. October 24, 2025."><strong>FDA approved October 2025</strong></Cite>

- Dual NK1/NK3 receptor antagonist—blocks two pathways
- <Cite id="elinzanetant-oasis" tooltip="Pinkerton JV, et al. Elinzanetant for the Treatment of Vasomotor Symptoms Associated With Menopause: OASIS 1 and 2 Randomized Clinical Trials. JAMA. 2024;332(16):1343-1354.">About two-thirds fewer moderate-to-severe hot flashes by week 12 in postmenopausal women (placebo brought a bit under half, which is why the trials matter)</Cite>
- May also help [sleep](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come) and possibly [mood](/symptoms/mood) (it affects substance P, which is involved in pain and stress)
- Can cause drowsiness—which is a feature if you have insomnia
- Once daily, at bedtime
- **Important:** <Cite id="lynkuet-label-2026" tooltip="Bayer HealthCare Pharmaceuticals. LYNKUET (elinzanetant) Prescribing Information. DailyMed.">The FDA label calls for baseline liver tests before you start</Cite>
- <Cite id="elinzanetant-oasis4-2025" tooltip="Cardoso F, et al. Elinzanetant for Vasomotor Symptoms from Endocrine Therapy for Breast Cancer. New England Journal of Medicine. 2025;393(8):753-763.">Also reduced hot flashes caused by breast-cancer endocrine therapy (tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors) in a major trial—though that use isn't yet FDA-approved in the US</Cite>

### What's Coming

**Cendifensine** is in Phase 2 trials. It works on both monoamine and KNDy pathways, and may address mood, food cravings, and hot flashes together. We'll know more as trials progress.

## Antidepressants That Help Hot Flashes

Some antidepressants—SSRIs and SNRIs—reduce hot flash frequency through mechanisms we don't fully understand. They're not as effective as estrogen or the NK3 antagonists, but they help some women.

### Paroxetine (Brisdelle)

- The only SSRI FDA-approved specifically for [hot flashes](/symptoms/hot-flashes)
- Low dose (7.5mg)—lower than antidepressant doses
- Modest effect—<Cite id="brisdelle-label-2025" tooltip="Legacy Pharma USA. BRISDELLE (paroxetine) Prescribing Information. DailyMed.">the FDA label reports about 1 to 2 fewer hot flashes a day than placebo at week 12.</Cite> <Cite id="paroxetine-simon-2013" tooltip="Simon JA, et al. Low-Dose Paroxetine 7.5 mg for Menopausal Vasomotor Symptoms: Two Randomized Controlled Trials. Menopause. 2013;20(10):1027-1035.">Two phase 3 trials in postmenopausal women found it significantly better than placebo.</Cite>
- Side effects can include nausea, headache, sexual dysfunction
- **Important:** Do not use with tamoxifen (drug interaction)

### Venlafaxine (Effexor)

- SNRI used off-label for hot flashes
- <Cite id="ssri-hot-flashes" tooltip="Loprinzi CL, et al. Venlafaxine in Management of Hot Flashes in Survivors of Breast Cancer: A Randomised Controlled Trial. Lancet. 2000;356(9247):2059-2063.">Reduced hot flash scores by about 60% at 75 mg or more in a 4-week randomized trial of women with a history of, or fear of, breast cancer, versus 27% with placebo</Cite>
- May also help [mood symptoms](/symptoms/mood) at higher doses
- Can be difficult to stop (discontinuation syndrome)

### Other Antidepressants

Escitalopram (Lexapro), citalopram (Celexa), and desvenlafaxine (Pristiq) have some evidence as well. If you're considering an antidepressant for mood anyway, discussing one with hot flash benefits makes sense.

## Other Medications

### Gabapentin

- Anticonvulsant used off-label for hot flashes
- <Cite id="gabapentin-hot-flashes" tooltip="Guttuso T Jr, et al. Gabapentin's Effects on Hot Flashes in Postmenopausal Women: A Randomized Controlled Trial. Obstetrics & Gynecology. 2003;101(2):337-345.">Reduced hot flash frequency by 45% versus 29% with placebo in a randomized trial</Cite>
- Particularly helpful for nighttime symptoms
- May improve [sleep](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come) as a side benefit
- Side effects: drowsiness, dizziness, weight gain
- Often taken at bedtime

### Oxybutynin

- Originally for overactive bladder
- Repurposed for hot flashes (works on sweating)
- Side effects typical of anticholinergics (dry mouth, constipation)
- Sometimes used when other options haven't worked

### Clonidine

- Blood pressure medication
- Modest effect on hot flashes
- Side effects (low blood pressure, drowsiness) limit usefulness for many women
- Not usually a first choice

### Ospemifene (Osphena)

- For [vaginal symptoms](/symptoms/dryness) specifically, not hot flashes
- Selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM)—acts like estrogen on vaginal tissue
- Oral tablet
- Option for women who can't use vaginal estrogen
- May cause hot flashes as a side effect (ironic, I know)

## How to Think About These Options

The NK3 antagonists are the most exciting development for women who can't or don't want to take hormones. They target a pathway research has implicated in hot flashes, rather than just masking symptoms.

Antidepressants make sense when you have both [mood symptoms](/symptoms/mood) and hot flashes. You might address two problems with one medication.

Gabapentin is worth considering if your hot flashes are worst at night and you're also struggling with [sleep](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come).

For [vaginal symptoms](/symptoms/dryness), ospemifene or vaginal moisturizers and lubricants are options—though for most women, local vaginal estrogen (which has minimal systemic absorption) is still the most effective treatment.

## What NAMS/NCCIH Does NOT Recommend

For completeness, here's what major medical organizations advise against:

- **Custom-compounded "bioidentical" hormones** — Quality and consistency issues; not better than FDA-approved bioidentical options
- **DHEA supplements** (for systemic use) — Limited evidence, unclear safety (though vaginal DHEA is approved for vaginal symptoms)
- **Wild yam creams** — Your body cannot convert the compounds in wild yam to progesterone
- **Kava** — Hepatotoxicity (liver damage) risk

---

None of these options is perfect. All have trade-offs. But having options matters—especially if [hormone therapy](/support/hormone-therapy) isn't right for you.

Discuss these with a [provider who understands menopause](/finding-care). What works depends on your specific symptoms, medical history, and preferences.
