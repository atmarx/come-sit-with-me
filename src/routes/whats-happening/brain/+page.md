If you've ever been mid-conversation when the heat rose through you like a wave—sudden, fierce, leaving you flushed and wondering if anyone noticed—I want you to know: we finally have a real lead on why.

For decades, hot flashes were dismissed as mysterious, psychosomatic, "just something that happens." But science has started catching up to what your body already knew. And the best explanation so far points to a tiny cluster of neurons deep in your brain.

## The Discovery That Changed Everything

In the early 2000s, researchers discovered a group of neurons in the hypothalamus that they named **KNDy neurons** (for kisspeptin, neurokinin B, and dynorphin—the signaling molecules they use).

These neurons signal directly to your brain's thermostat, as far as the research can tell. And they have estrogen receptors.

Here's what the research suggests:

<Cite id="kndy-thermoregulation-2013" tooltip="Rance NE, et al. Modulation of Body Temperature and LH Secretion by Hypothalamic KNDy Neurons: A Novel Hypothesis on the Mechanism of Hot Flushes. Frontiers in Neuroendocrinology. 2013;34(3):211-227.">They appear to relay estrogen signals to your temperature control center: women with hot flashes have a lower threshold for sweating, and in rats, losing estrogen shifts the comfort zone downward.</Cite> <Cite id="kndy-circuit-mouse-2018" tooltip="Padilla SL, et al. A Neural Circuit Underlying the Generation of Hot Flushes. Cell Reports. 2018;24(2):271-277.">And in a mouse study, brief activation of these neurons after sex hormones were withdrawn contributed to hot flashes, through the release of neurokinin B.</Cite>

Normally, your body tolerates a range of temperatures before deciding you're too hot or too cold. With a lower threshold, a change that wouldn't have registered before can trigger your body's full heat-dissipation response: blood vessels dilate, sweat pours, your skin flushes.

That's the best picture we have of a hot flash. It's not in your head. As far as we can tell, it's in your hypothalamus.

## Why This Matters for Understanding Your Experience

This line of research may help explain things we couldn't explain before:

- **Why withdrawal matters more than level**: It's not just low estrogen that causes symptoms—it's the *drop*. Rapid fluctuations are more symptomatic than stable low estrogen. This is why early perimenopause, when hormones swing wildly, can feel worse than being firmly postmenopausal.

- **Why some women never get hot flashes**: Individual variation in these neurons and their sensitivity to estrogen means some women sail through while others are drenched nightly. Neither is imagining anything.

- **Why new medications work**: There's a new class of drugs (NK3 receptor antagonists) that can reduce hot flashes without providing any estrogen at all, by blocking a receptor in this same neurokinin pathway. That's good reason to take the pathway seriously. (More in [Support Options](/support/beyond-hormones).)

## The Mood Connection: Emerging Research

Here's something else we're learning—and I'll be honest that the research is still emerging, but it's too important not to share:

**FSH itself may directly affect mood.**

We used to think depression and anxiety during perimenopause were simply due to estrogen changes. But newer studies (2024-2025) suggest that rising FSH—independent of estrogen levels—correlates with depressive symptoms.

What we know:
- <Cite id="swan-major-depression-2011" tooltip="Bromberger JT, et al. Major Depression During and After the Menopausal Transition: Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN). Psychological Medicine. 2011;41(9):1879-1888.">In one SWAN site's study of 221 women, major depression was two to four times more likely during perimenopause or early postmenopause than before it</Cite>
- The longer your perimenopause lasts, the higher your risk
- Interestingly, a *rapid* rise in FSH may actually be protective—a shorter transition seems easier on mood
- <Cite id="anxiety-perimenopause" tooltip="Bromberger JT, Epperson CN. Depression During and After the Perimenopause: Impact of Hormones, Genetics, and Environmental Determinants of Disease. Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinics of North America. 2018;45(4):663-678.">Women with a history of severe premenstrual mood symptoms are at higher risk of depression during perimenopause</Cite>

### The Estradiol Variability Hypothesis

Some researchers now believe it's not the *level* of estrogen that affects mood, but how sensitive you are to *changes* in estrogen. The direction, the speed, the magnitude of change.

This explains why:
- The chaotic transition is often harder than stable postmenopause
- Stabilizing hormone levels—even at low levels—often helps mood
- What helped for PMS might help now
- Your friend with similar hormone levels might feel completely different

## What This Means for You

Your [brain fog](/symptoms/brain-fog) is real. Your [mood changes](/symptoms/mood) are real. Your [sleep disruption](/symptoms/sleep-wont-come) is real. These aren't character flaws or failures of will. They're your brain responding to a profound neurochemical shift.

Understanding this doesn't make symptoms disappear. But it can help you stop questioning yourself. It can help you make informed choices about [support strategies](/support). And it can help you explain to others—partners, doctors, yourself—that what you're experiencing has a biological basis.

Your brain is adapting to a new hormonal reality. It takes time. And you deserve support while it does.
